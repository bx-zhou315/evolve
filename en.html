Thinking Guide

Feeling lost at the table is usually not about missing theory – it's about not knowing what to ask yourself first at each decision. This guide keeps what you must remember to a minimum and gives a fixed thinking order for every street. Read it before you play; at the table, use only the decision flow.

10 things to remember

  1. Position matters more than cards. The later you act, the more hands you can play; out of position, play the same hands tighter.
  2. Play preflop from the charts. Preflop is the only street you can fully memorise. Opening, facing a raise, facing a 3bet – check the chart, don't improvise.
  3. Don't limp. When nobody has raised, either raise or fold (skip the special small-blind strategies for now).
  4. Think about the opponent's range, not one hand. Ask "which hands would play like this?", not "does he have AK?".
  5. Equity needed = call amount ÷ total pot after calling. Pot 100, opponent bets 50: you call 50 to win 200, so you need 25%.
  6. Minimum defence frequency MDF = pot ÷ (pot + bet). Against a half-pot bet your whole range must defend at least 67%, or he profits by bluffing with anything.
  7. Small bets go with high frequency, big bets with polarisation. Bet small and often with a clear range advantage on dry boards; bet big only with nuts and air.
  8. Every bet needs a reason: value, bluff (semi-bluff) or protection. If you can't name the reason, check.
  9. Medium hands check and call more, and bet less. When you bet, worse hands fold and better hands call, so medium hands lose both ways.
  10. Judge decisions, not results. Losing a hand doesn't mean you played it wrong, and winning doesn't mean you played it right. Look at EV.

Thinking order for every decision (at the table, about 10 seconds)

1
Where am I, and who has the initiative?

My position (in position IP / out of position OOP); who made the last raise on the previous street (the aggressor). The aggressor's range is usually stronger, so he does more of the betting.

2
What is the opponent's range?

Replay each of his actions from preflop. Which position did he open from, did he 3bet; on earlier streets did he bet, call or check? A check usually means no very strong hand (a "capped" range).

3
Who does this board favour?

High dry boards (A♠7♦2♣) favour the preflop raiser; low connected boards (7♠6♦5♣) favour the caller; when flushes or straights complete, ask who is more likely to hold those combos.

4
Which category is my hand within my own range?

Strong made hand, medium made hand, draw or air. The default action for each category is in the street tables.

5
Pick the action, then the size.

Nobody has bet: bet value and bluffs, check medium hands. Facing a bet: work out the equity you need first, then see if your hand is good enough against his betting range.

Defaults when you can't work it out: in position, lean towards checking to control the pot; out of position, lean towards check-calling or folding. Don't take risky raises with medium hands.

Turn

On the turn ask one question: what did this card change?

  • Who does it help? High cards (A, K) usually help the preflop raiser; small and middle cards that connect the board help the caller.
  • Did a draw complete? When a third flush card or a straight card arrives, the side less likely to have it should slow down.
  • Did my hand improve or get worse? Top pair on the flop can become a medium hand once a higher card comes on the turn.
  • Is the reason to keep betting still there? Keep betting value; keep semi-bluffing draws with outs; hands that bluffed the flop and picked up nothing on the turn usually give up.
  • Stack depth: after a turn bet, the river is often an all-in. Decide what you plan to do on the river before choosing your turn size.

River: five steps

  1. Rebuild the line and write down the opponent's range.
  2. Compare ranges: range advantage is about who is stronger overall; nut advantage is about who has more of the strongest hands.
  3. Place your hand: value, medium (bluff-catcher) or air. There are no draws on the river – you win or you lose.
  4. When nobody has bet: bet value to get called by worse; bluff air to fold out better; check medium hands to showdown. Big sizes for polarised ranges, small sizes for merged ones.
  5. Facing a bet: work out the equity needed and MDF, then look at blockers. Bluff-catchers that block his value hands are better calls.

When to deviate from GTO

GTO is a way of playing that can't be exploited when you don't know who your opponent is. Once you know him, you can adjust to his leaks – but only ones you have actually observed, not guessed.

You observe that the opponent…Adjustment
Calls too much and rarely foldsBluff less; value-bet thinner (bet medium hands too)
Folds too muchBluff more, especially after he checks
Rarely bluffs with big river betsFold medium bluff-catchers more against his big bets
Almost never raises after checkingBet more boldly; even medium hands can bet small
Calls wide preflop and plays passively after the flopRaise strong hands to isolate him, and value-bet more after the flop

Many people think low-stakes live players are passive overall and under-bluff the river, but every table is different – watch a few hands before drawing conclusions.

Mindset and bankroll

  • Only play with money you can afford to lose. A common guideline is at least 30 to 50 buy-ins for cash games, but that's a rule of thumb, not a guarantee.
  • Losing streaks are when tilt hits hardest. Set a stop-loss, for example leave after losing 3 buy-ins in a session.
  • Note down hands you weren't sure about and review them at home with this app's custom scenarios, rather than just remembering "I lost".
  • Poker has a large element of luck; short-term results don't show your level. Look at your decision quality over the long run.

How to study with this app

  1. Week 1, preflop: do 50 preflop questions a day until your opening-range accuracy is steadily above 90%.
  2. Week 2, flop: practise by board type on the Flop page, compare with the board table above, and understand why some boards bet more and others check more.
  3. Week 3, turn: play full hands (turn to river) on the Turn page, focusing on "what did this card change?".
  4. Week 4, river: practise the five steps in Guided mode and check which step has the lowest accuracy in your training log.
  5. After that: switch to "Random each hand" for mixed practice, and check the leak finder and confidence calibration once a week.

Five-step river analysis
  1. Rebuild the line and write down the opponent’s range.Ask yourself: given what the opponent did on each street, which hands play like this? Remove hands that would have folded preflop, raised the flop or bet the turn.
  2. Compare the two ranges.Range advantage is about whose range is stronger overall; nut advantage is about who has more of the very strongest hands. The side with the nut advantage can bet big or even overbet; the other side checks and defends more.
  3. Place your own hand.Compare it with the opponent’s current range: high equity means value, the middle is showdown value or a bluff-catcher, very low is air. The same hand can change category on different boards and lines.
  4. When nobody has bet, pick a goal, then a size.Betting value aims to get called by worse hands; betting air is a bluff that aims to fold out better hands. Betting a medium hand usually achieves neither, so check it more. Use big sizes with a polarised range (only strong hands and air) and small sizes with a merged range (lots of medium-strong hands).
  5. Facing a bet, work out the price first, then blockers.Equity needed to call = call amount ÷ total pot after calling; minimum defence frequency MDF = pot ÷ (pot + bet). Compare your hand with the opponent’s betting range, not his whole range. Hands that block his value hands are better calls; hands that block his bluffs are better folds. If you can raise: raise for value when worse hands will still call; raise as a bluff with hands that can’t win at showdown but block his strong hands; just call with medium hands.

"Guided" mode turns steps 2, 3 and 4/5 of each hand into multiple-choice questions and checks them against the solver straight away; only the last step is the actual decision.

What this solver simplifies

River: Up to three bet sizes per player (anything over 70% of the remaining stack becomes all-in); facing a bet you can fold, call or raise to 3x; facing a raise you can only fold or call.

Turn: The turn has two bet sizes and one raise; then each of the 48 possible rivers is solved, but with only one 75% bet size and no raises on the river, or the browser could not cope. So river decisions inside turn hands are coarser than in pure river hands.

The built-in ranges are simplified, hand-written ranges for each line, not real preflop-to-turn solver output. The point is to practise the way of thinking, not to memorise exact frequencies. River exploitability is usually within 0.05% of the pot, turn within 0.3%. For offline training only; do not use it live in real-money games.
